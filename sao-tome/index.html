Background · Chapter 01

The Islands

Two volcanic islands on the equator, with five centuries of history, a creole culture of their own and rainforest found nowhere else.

Pico Cão Grande · Wikimedia Commons ↗
At a glance

Africa's second-smallest nation

São Tomé and Príncipe, officially the Democratic Republic of São Tomé and Príncipe, is an island country in the Gulf of Guinea, off the western equatorial coast of Central Africa. It is made up of two archipelagos around the main islands of São Tomé and Príncipe, which lie about 150 km apart and roughly 250 and 225 km off the northwest coast of Gabon.

Only the Seychelles is smaller or less populous in Africa. Since the democratic reforms of 1990 the country has been one of the continent's most stable democracies. Its culture, music and food mix African and Portuguese influences, and it was a founding member of the Community of Portuguese Language Countries.

Geography & climate

Born from the Cameroon Line

Both islands are part of the Cameroon volcanic line, a chain that also includes Bioko, Annobón and Mount Cameroon. They rose from deep water about 30 million years ago, and their soils of basalt and phonolite have fed plantation crops since colonial times.

São Tomé is about 50 km long and 30 km wide, and it is the more mountainous of the two islands. Príncipe is about 30 km long and 6 km wide, and its highest peak reaches 948 m. The equator runs just south of São Tomé, through the islet of Ilhéu das Rolas.

At sea level the climate is hot and humid, averaging about 26 °C, and it rarely goes above 32 °C. The highlands average around 20 °C, with cool nights. Rainfall ranges from about 800 mm a year in the northern lowlands to 7,000 mm in the highland cloud forest. The rainy season runs from October to May.

The country has seven districts. São Tomé island has six: Água Grande, Cantagalo, Caué, Lembá, Lobata and Mé-Zóchi. Príncipe has been an autonomous region since 1995, and its single district is Pagué.

History

Five centuries in brief

The islands' past includes the Atlantic slave trade and forced labour on the plantations. Visitors should treat these places with the care they deserve.

  1. 1470

    Portuguese navigators João de Santarém and Pêro Escobar reach the islands, which were uninhabited, on 21 December.

  2. 1493

    Álvaro Caminha founds the first lasting settlement on São Tomé. Príncipe is settled in 1500.

  3. 1500s

    Sugar mills spread across São Tomé and it becomes Africa's leading sugar exporter. The whole economy runs on enslaved labour, and the islands become a major depot of the Atlantic slave trade.

  4. 1595

    Amador leads the largest slave revolt in the islands' history. About 5,000 people rise up and destroy 60 of the island's 85 sugar mills before the revolt is put down.

  5. 1800s

    Coffee and cocoa are introduced. The roças, vast plantation estates, come to dominate the land and the people who work it.

  6. 1905

    São Tomé is the world's largest producer of cocoa, and the islands become known as the Chocolate Islands. Portugal abolished slavery in 1876, but forced contract labour continues.

  7. 1919

    On Príncipe, Sir Arthur Eddington photographs a total solar eclipse and provides one of the first successful tests of Einstein's general theory of relativity.

  8. 1953

    Several hundred African labourers are killed in the Batepá Massacre. The anniversary is still officially observed.

  9. 1975

    Independence on 12 July, with Manuel Pinto da Costa of the MLSTP as the first president.

  10. 1990–91

    A new constitution legalises opposition parties, and the 1991 elections are free, peaceful and transparent. São Tomé is one of the first African countries to make this transition.

  11. 2012 → 2025

    UNESCO names the Island of Príncipe Biosphere Reserve. In 2025 it is extended to São Tomé, and the country becomes the first to be designated in its entirety.

People & culture

African roots, Portuguese echoes

Most São Toméans are of African and mestiço (mixed African and European) descent. Among the islands' communities are the Forros, descendants of people freed when slavery was abolished; the Angolares, who by tradition descend from survivors of a 1540 shipwreck and still live by fishing; the Serviçais, contract workers from Angola, Mozambique and Cape Verde; and the Tongas, their island-born children.

Portuguese is the official language and is spoken by about 98% of people. Forro creole is spoken by roughly a third of the population, and Angolar and Principense creoles survive alongside it. Around 72% of people are Catholic.

Performance

Tchiloli & Danço-Congo

Tchiloli is a masked dance-drama that tells a story of justice and power through music and dance. The Danço-Congo likewise combines music, dance and theatre.

Music

Ússua, Socopé, Dêxa

São Tomé is known for the ússua and socopé rhythms, and Príncipe for the dêxa beat. Portuguese ballroom dance probably helped shape all three.

Literature

A poetic tradition

The islands' Portuguese-language poetry is among the richest in Lusophone Africa. Key voices include Francisco José Tenreiro, Alda Espírito Santo, Manuela Margarido, Olinda Beja and Conceição Lima.

Cuisine

Fish, breadfruit & spice

Staple foods are fish and seafood, beans, maize, breadfruit and cooked banana, with pineapple and avocado and plenty of hot spice. Coffee is even used as a seasoning.

Everyday life

Leve-leve

“Slowly, slowly.” It is less a slogan than a way of living. Football is the most popular sport, and the national team plays in CAF and FIFA competitions.

Heritage

The roças

Former plantations such as Agostinho Neto, Monte Café and Sundy had their own hospitals, railways and workers' villages. Many are now being reused for heritage tourism.

Nature & wildlife

An evolutionary laboratory

The islands belong to the São Tomé, Príncipe and Annobón moist lowland forests ecoregion. Mammals are few, although the São Tomé shrew and several bats are endemic. Birds and plants are another story. The islands are home to the world's smallest ibis (the São Tomé ibis), the world's largest sunbird (the giant sunbird), the rare São Tomé fiscal and several giant species of Begonia.

Beaches on both islands are important nesting sites for marine turtles, including the critically endangered hawksbill turtle. Much of the mountainous interior is protected as Obô Natural Park, and since 2025 the whole country has been a UNESCO Biosphere Reserve.

When to go

Two seasons, both green

The islands are warm all year, around 26 °C on the coast. The rainy season runs from October to May. The drier, slightly cooler months from June to September are known locally as the gravana, and they are the best time for hiking and clear views of the peaks. A short, drier spell around December and January (the gravanito) is another good window.

Wildlife sets its own calendar. Turtle nesting is concentrated in the wetter months, roughly November to March, and humpback whales pass the islands from about July to October.

Transportation

Getting there & around

International flights land at São Tomé International Airport, near the capital, which has been expanded and modernised. Most visitors connect through Lisbon or regional hubs such as Luanda and Libreville. Routes and airlines change from season to season, so check current schedules before you book.

The country's main seaports are in São Tomé city and Neves. Both were modernised in 2014 and mainly handle cargo. Visitors travelling between the two islands usually take the short domestic flight.

Between islands

São Tomé → Príncipe

Short domestic flights connect the two islands several times a week. Seats are limited, so book early and allow a spare day in case of changes.

On the road

Taxis & drivers

Shared and private taxis are the everyday way to get around. Hiring a driver-guide for a day is good value and puts your money in local hands. Agree the fare before you set off.

Self-drive

Car & 4×4 hire

The road network is good by regional standards, but the far south and forest tracks are much easier in a 4×4, especially in the rainy season.

By sea

Boats & islets

Local boats run to Ilhéu das Rolas from the south of São Tomé and to the islets around Príncipe. Use operators who carry life jackets.

On foot

Guided trails

Obô Natural Park, Lagoa Amélia and Pico de São Tomé should be done with a local guide. It is safer, and it is income for the community.

Connectivity

Phones & internet

Mobile phones are widely used and coverage keeps improving. Internet is reliable in towns but patchy in the interior, so download offline maps before you go.

Beaches

Sand, palms & volcanic rock

Both islands are ringed with quiet beaches, from the palm-fringed crescents of Príncipe to the black-rock coves of São Tomé. Many have no facilities at all, so bring water and shade, and take everything home with you.

Accommodation

Where to stay

Hotels on the bay, from large international properties such as the Pestana São Tomé to small family guesthouses. The city is a short drive from the airport and the natural base for your first and last nights.

Before you go

Practical essentials

Currency
Dobra (STN). This is a largely cash economy. Carry euros, which are easy to exchange, because card acceptance outside large hotels is limited.
Language
Portuguese is spoken almost everywhere, alongside Forro, Angolar and Principense creoles. French and English are taught in schools. A few Portuguese phrases go a long way.
Entry
Many nationalities can visit visa-free for short stays, and others apply for an e-visa. Rules change, so check the official requirements for your passport before you travel.
Health
A yellow fever vaccination certificate is normally required, and malaria is present. Speak to a travel clinic about prevention and travel insurance before you leave.
Time & dialling
UTC+0 (GMT) all year · country code +239 · internet domain .st
Safety
One of Africa's most stable and peaceful countries. Take normal care with valuables in the city.
Etiquette
Greet people, take your time, and always ask before photographing someone. Dress modestly away from the beach. Leve-leve applies to service too.

    Sustainability · Chapter 03

    Tourism & the SDGs

    How visitors can create decent local jobs, protect turtles and rainforest, and how technology, used carefully, can help.

    Fishing beach, São Tomé · Wikimedia Commons ↗
    The challenge

    Why tourism matters here

    Since the 19th century the islands' economy has depended on plantation agriculture, especially cocoa. The country imports most of its food; in 1997 about 90% of its food needs were met by imports. The biggest economic challenges it faces are a small workforce, a tiny domestic market, the cost of being an archipelago, climatic fluctuations, global warming and poverty.

    Tourism is widely seen as the sector with the greatest potential to diversify the economy. Foreign investment is growing and new lodges are opening. The question this project asks is how that growth can reach families in Neves, Angolares and Santo António, and protect the forests and beaches that visitors come for.

    UN Sustainable Development Goals

    The goals this project supports

    8SDG
    Primary goal

    Decent Work & Economic Growth

    Target 8.9 calls for policies that promote sustainable tourism which creates jobs and promotes local culture and products. In São Tomé that means guides, drivers, cooks, craftspeople and roça staff earning directly from visitors, instead of the money leaving the islands through imports and foreign-owned packages.

    • Visitors: hire certified local guides, and buy island chocolate, coffee and crafts.
    • Businesses: train and promote local staff, and buy from local farmers and fishers.
    14SDG

    Life Below Water

    The islands' beaches are nesting grounds for marine turtles, including the critically endangered hawksbill, and the surrounding sea supports the fishing communities. Paid turtle-watching gives a nesting female more value alive than the income she once brought to a hunter.

    • Visitors: join guided night walks only, use red light, and never buy turtle-shell products.
    • Operators: fund beach patrols and hire former hunters as guardians.
    15SDG

    Life on Land

    Obô Natural Park protects endemic species such as the giant sunbird and the São Tomé ibis. Park fees and guided hikes give local people a steady income from keeping the forest standing.

    • Visitors: stay on the trails, pay park fees and carry your rubbish out.
    • Planners: set visitor limits for fragile sites such as Lagoa Amélia.
    12SDG

    Responsible Consumption & Production

    Target 12.b is about developing tools to monitor the effects of sustainable tourism. For a small island state, simple digital monitoring of visitor numbers, spending and waste turns good intentions into evidence.

    • Visitors: bring a refillable bottle and avoid single-use plastic.
    • Government: publish open data on visitor numbers and where their money goes.
    Community & environment

    How tourism can help

    Bringing the roças back to life. Restoring plantation buildings as guesthouses, museums and restaurants keeps heritage alive and creates year-round jobs in villages that grew up around the estates.

    Adding value to cocoa. A bag of beans exported in bulk earns little. A tasting, a chocolate workshop or a bar made on the island earns many times more, and it tells visitors the story of the Chocolate Islands.

    Buying local food. When hotels buy from local farmers and fishers, the country imports less, farm incomes rise and guests eat better.

    Paying for conservation. Park fees, guide wages and turtle-watching income make conservation pay. Forest and beaches then earn more protected than cleared.

    Cultural pride. Audiences for Tchiloli, socopé and creole literature give young São Toméans a reason to learn these traditions and a way to earn from them.

    Tech-enabled solutions

    Practical ideas for sustainable growth

    1. AI trip personalisationSDG 8 · visibility

      Itinerary tools, like the planner on this site, that deliberately route visitors to community stays, guides and restaurants and spread them across districts and seasons, not just the few best-known beaches.

    2. Multilingual AI conciergeInclusivity

      A chatbot on WhatsApp that answers in Portuguese, English and French, runs on low bandwidth, and hands bookings to local operators who have no website of their own.

    3. Community booking & mobile paymentsSDG 8 · fair income

      One shared, low-fee listing for guides, homestays and drivers, paid by mobile money, so payments reach families and are not taken by foreign platforms.

    4. AI-assisted wildlife monitoringSDG 14 & 15

      Camera traps and drone surveys analysed by AI to count turtle nests and detect forest loss, giving rangers data they can act on and giving visitors evidence of the impact of their fees.

    5. QR heritage trailsCulture

      QR codes at roças, forts and churches linking to short audio stories told by local voices, in Forro as well as Portuguese and English, so heritage can earn income without adding new buildings.

    6. Visitor-impact dashboardSDG 12.b

      Open data on arrivals, local spending, waste and pressure on each site, used to set limits for fragile places such as Lagoa Amélia before damage is done.

    Critical assessment

    What could go wrong

    Internet is good in towns but patchy in the interior, and many small operators have limited digital skills. Global booking platforms can take a large share of the money, and data about communities can be collected without consent.

    How to get it right

    Design principles

    Build offline-first, low-bandwidth tools. Train local operators and give them ownership of their listings and data. Keep fees low, and measure success by the income that stays on the islands, not by clicks.
