## What we understand you are trying to fix

Your site has been running since 2016. The work it describes has changed a great deal since then, and the site has not kept up with it.

Reading it closely, three things stand out.

The first is that civil rights testing is buried. It is the method behind every programme you run, it is the reason your findings stand up in front of regulators and courts, and a first time visitor has to dig to find any explanation of it. It should be one of the first things the site says.

The second is that the site asks visitors to understand how the ERC is organised before it will help them. Someone who has just been turned away from an apartment does not know whether their problem is Fair Housing or Public Accommodations. They should not have to work that out before they can tell you what happened.

The third is accessibility, and this is the one that matters most. You are a civil rights organisation with a disability rights practice. More than 65,000 retail locations, restaurants and homes have been made more accessible because of your work. Your own site should be the clearest possible demonstration of what you ask of everyone else.

Your RFP scores accessibility on its own. We read that as a signal that you have had proposals before which treated it as a checkbox at the end. We have built ours the other way round.

This is the area your RFP weights separately, so it deserves evidence rather than assurances.

We do not assume contrast ratios, we measure them in the finished page. On the concept we built for you (see section 6) every text and background pair was checked against its computed value in a live browser. Body copy came out at 9.14:1, headings and navigation at 15.97:1, accent links at 6.10:1, button text at 6.81:1. WCAG AA asks for 4.5:1 on body text. Most of those pairs clear AAA.

We also test structure, not just colour. One `h1` per page, no skipped heading levels, no images without alt text, no unlabelled buttons, a working skip link, and a document language set.

## 3. Approach and design philosophy

### The idea

Your method is evidence. Two testers, matched on everything that should matter, differing on the one thing that should not. Side by side, a difference in treatment either exists or it does not.

The site should work the same way. Clear, specific, and confident enough not to shout. We would like it to feel closer to an investigative record than to a charity appeal, because that is what your work actually is.

### How we would run it

**Discovery is short and specific.** We do not need six weeks of workshops. We need your current analytics, a look at what people actually search for when they land on you, conversations with the intake coordinator and the fair housing team about the questions people ask before they file, and a clear view from you on which actions matter most.

**Information architecture comes before visual design.** The single biggest improvement available to you is not a new look. It is that a visitor with a problem can get to the right place in one step without understanding your internal structure. We would map journeys for the audiences you actually serve: someone reporting discrimination, a prospective tester, a housing provider looking for training, a funder, and a journalist on deadline.

**Accessibility is designed in, not audited at the end.** Every template gets a keyboard only pass. Every form validates inline with errors announced to screen readers, not silently marked in red. Motion respects `prefers-reduced-motion`, and translucency respects `prefers-reduced-transparency`. Your existing high contrast and larger text controls stay, and we would improve them so the setting persists between visits.

**Content is rewritten where it needs to be, not just moved.** Plain language matters more on your site than on most. Someone who has just been discriminated against is not in a frame of mind to parse legal phrasing.

### Platform recommendation

**We recommend staying on WordPress, with a custom block based theme.**

We are aware that recommending the platform you already use is the less impressive answer. It is still the right one, for three reasons.

Your staff already know it, so training cost is low and the site keeps getting updated after launch, which is where most nonprofit redesigns quietly fail. You already have a maintenance relationship with MightyMinnow, and your RFP asks us to confer with them on transition, which is far simpler if the platform does not change underneath them. And the form and CRM integrations you depend on are well supported there.

A custom block theme gives your team real editing freedom inside a design system that cannot be broken by accident. That is the combination that matters: flexible for editors, impossible to make ugly.

If you would prefer to move off WordPress, we will price that honestly as an alternative. We do not think it serves you.
